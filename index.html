Study of Binomial Distribution

Discrete probability model

Brief history

The binomial model developed from early studies of games of chance. Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat helped establish probability theory in the 1650s. Jacob Bernoulli’s Ars Conjectandi, published posthumously in 1713, developed repeated-trial probability ideas. The model describes the count of successes in a fixed number of independent trials.

Three real-life applications

  • Quality control: defective items in a fixed-size sample.
  • Healthcare: patients responding to a treatment in a study group, assuming a common success probability.
  • Digital systems: successful packet deliveries among a fixed number of independent transmission attempts.

Fixed n; two outcomes per trial; independent trials; constant success probability p.

Poisson Distribution

Discrete probability model

History and development

The distribution is named after French mathematician Siméon Denis Poisson, who described it in his 1837 work on probability. It later became known for modelling counts of relatively rare events. In 1898, Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz demonstrated its usefulness in analysing deaths caused by horse kicks in Prussian army corps.

Core idea: count how many events occur in a fixed interval of time, space, area, or volume when events occur independently at a stable average rate.

Three real-life examples

  • Service systems: number of customer calls arriving at a help desk in one minute.
  • Quality control: number of tiny defects on a fixed length of fabric or sheet of material.
  • Public services: number of emergency requests received by a control room in a fixed time interval.

These are suitable models when the event rate is reasonably stable and occurrences are approximately independent.

Distribution and parameters

P(X = k) = e−λ λk / k!

Support: k = 0, 1, 2, …

Parameter: λ > 0, the expected event count per chosen interval.

Theoretical meanE(X) = λ
Theoretical varianceVar(X) = λ

Standard deviation = √λ. For a Poisson process, counts in disjoint intervals are independent.

HyperGeometric Distribution

Discrete probability model · Sampling without replacement

History and development

The hypergeometric distribution grew out of classical probability problems involving finite collections and selection without replacement. Its mathematical foundations developed during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries through work on combinatorics and probability, including the work of Blaise Pascal, Pierre de Fermat, and later probability theorists. It is now a standard model for finite-population sampling.

Core idea: count the number of “successes” in a fixed-size sample drawn from a finite population, without putting selected items back.

Three real-life examples

  • Quality inspection: count defective items in a sample taken from a fixed production lot.
  • Card selection: count how many hearts appear in a hand drawn from a standard deck.
  • Inventory audit: count expired products in a sample selected from a finite stock without replacement.

The key condition is sampling without replacement, so the draws are dependent.

Distribution and parameters

P(X = x) = C(M,x) C(N−M,n−x) / C(N,n)

N: population size · M: number of success items · n: sample size.

Support: max(0, n−(N−M)) ≤ x ≤ min(n,M).

Geometric Distribution

Discrete waiting-time model

History and development

The geometric distribution belongs to the early development of probability theory in the seventeenth century. Its foundations are linked to Bernoulli trials and the study of repeated independent experiments with two outcomes. The term “geometric” reflects the geometric progression of successive probabilities, each multiplied by the same failure probability.

Core idea: model the trial number on which the first success occurs when independent trials share a constant success probability.

Three real-life examples

  • Quality testing: number of items inspected until the first defective item is found, when each item has a constant defect probability.
  • Communication: number of independent transmission attempts until the first successful delivery.
  • Customer response: number of independent contacts until the first positive response, under a stable response probability.

These examples assume independent trials and a constant probability of success.

Statistical model and parameters

P(X = k) = (1−p)k−1 p

Support: k = 1, 2, 3, …, where X is the trial of the first success.

Parameter: 0 < p < 1, probability of success on each trial.

Theoretical meanE(X) = 1/p
Theoretical varianceVar(X) = (1−p)/p²

The model has an unbounded positive-integer support; simulation displays a finite range capturing most of its probability.

Uniform Distribution

Continuous probability model

History and development

The uniform distribution reflects the classical idea of equal likelihood over a specified range. Its continuous form became part of the development of mathematical probability and calculus-based probability models in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. It is also a fundamental model in modern simulation, where uniform random numbers are used to generate samples from other distributions.

Core idea: every equal-length subinterval within [a,b] has the same probability; probability is proportional to interval length.

Three real-life examples

  • Random timing: a system chooses a delay uniformly between two fixed limits.
  • Simulation: a pseudo-random number generator produces values on [0,1) as a base for Monte Carlo methods.
  • Service scheduling: a start time is selected uniformly within a defined window when all times in that window are treated as equally likely.

These are modelling assumptions, not universal claims about all real-world timing or random-number systems.

Statistical distribution and parameters

f(x) = 1/(b−a),   a ≤ x ≤ b

Parameters: lower limit a and upper limit b, with a < b.

CDF: F(x) = 0 for x<a; (x−a)/(b−a) for a≤x≤b; 1 for x>b.

Theoretical mean(a+b)/2
Theoretical variance(b−a)²/12

For a continuous model, the probability at any single point is zero; probabilities are areas under the density curve.

Exponential distribution

Continuous waiting-time model

History and development

The exponential distribution emerged in the development of probability models for waiting times and event processes. It is closely connected to the Poisson process: if events occur independently at a constant rate, the time between successive events follows an exponential distribution. It became widely used in reliability engineering, queueing theory, and survival analysis.

Core idea: model the waiting time until the next event under a constant event rate.

Three real-life examples

  • Service systems: time between incoming calls when arrivals are approximated by a constant-rate Poisson process.
  • Reliability: lifetime of a component with a constant failure hazard rate.
  • Network systems: waiting time between independent packet arrivals under a stable traffic-rate model.

These examples rely on a constant-rate / memoryless model assumption; real systems may deviate from it.

Statistical distribution and parameters

f(x) = λe−λx,   x ≥ 0

Parameter: rate λ > 0. The scale (mean waiting time) is 1/λ.

CDF: F(x)=1−e−λx; survival function S(x)=e−λx.

Theoretical varianceVar(X)=1/λ²

Gaussian OR Normal Distribution

History

  • Abraham de Moivre described a Normal-curve approximation to the binomial distribution in 1733.
  • Pierre-Simon Laplace developed its use in probability and error theory in the late eighteenth century.
  • Carl Friedrich Gauss applied it to astronomical measurement and least-squares work around 1809, linking it closely with measurement errors.
  • Today it is central to statistics, scientific measurement, and the Central Limit Theorem.

Statistical Distribution and Parameters

A continuous random variable X follows a Normal distribution when its probability density has a symmetric, bell-shaped curve.

Probability density: f(x) = [1 / (σ √(2π))] × exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))
Location parameter: μ
Any real number; determines the centre.
Scale parameter: σ
Must be greater than 0; controls spread.

Standard Normal Distribution

When μ = 0 and σ = 1, the variable is standard normal, commonly denoted Z.

Z ~ N(0, 1) · Mean = 0 · Variance = 1

Used for Z-scores, probabilities, confidence intervals, and test statistics.

Three Real-Life Applications

1

Human measurements: adult heights within a suitably defined population are often approximately Normal.

2

Measurement errors: small random errors in repeated physical or laboratory measurements are often modelled using a Normal distribution.

3

Quality and natural variation: manufactured part dimensions, test scores, and some biological measurements can be approximately Normal under appropriate conditions.

These are useful models, not universal rules; check data before assuming normality.

Student's t Distribution

An exact sampling distribution used for inference about a population mean when the population variance is unknown

Distribution, History and Applications

History

The Student's t distribution was introduced by William Sealy Gosset, who published under the pseudonym “Student” in 1908. It arose from the problem of estimating a normal population mean using a sample standard deviation, especially for small samples.

Three real-life applications
  1. One-sample and paired-sample t tests for mean differences when the population variance is unknown.
  2. Confidence intervals for a normally distributed population mean, particularly with small samples.
  3. Regression coefficient tests and confidence intervals under the classical linear-model assumptions.

Probability density function

T = Z / √(U/ν), where Z ~ N(0,1), U ~ χ²(ν), independently; f(t) = Γ((ν+1)/2) / [√(νπ) Γ(ν/2)] · (1+t²/ν)^{−(ν+1)/2}

Degrees of freedom: For a sample of size n from a normal population, ν = n − 1. More generally, ν indexes the chi-square component in the normal/chi-square ratio.

F Distribution

An exact sampling distribution formed as the ratio of two independent chi-square variables divided by their degrees of freedom

Distribution, History and Applications

History

The F distribution is named after Sir Ronald A. Fisher. Its modern form was developed in the early twentieth century for variance-ratio methods and analysis of variance. George W. Snedecor popularised the F notation in honour of Fisher.

Three real-life applications
  1. Analysis of variance (ANOVA), comparing explained variation with within-group variation under model assumptions.
  2. Overall significance testing in linear regression and tests of nested linear models.
  3. Classical comparison of two independent normal-population variances through a variance ratio.

Probability density function

F = (U₁/d₁)/(U₂/d₂), U₁ ~ χ²(d₁), U₂ ~ χ²(d₂), independently; f(x) = [ (d₁/d₂)^{d₁/2} x^{d₁/2−1} ] / [ B(d₁/2,d₂/2) (1+d₁x/d₂)^{(d₁+d₂)/2} ], x>0

Degrees of freedom: Two positive integer degrees of freedom are required: d₁ for the numerator and d₂ for the denominator. Their roles are not interchangeable in general.

Chi-Square (χ²) Distribution

An exact sampling distribution generated by the sum of squares of independent standard normal variables

Distribution, History and Applications

History

The chi-square distribution was developed by Karl Pearson in the early twentieth century in connection with goodness-of-fit testing. It is also central to inference about normal-population variance and to contingency-table analysis.

Three real-life applications
  1. Pearson's chi-square goodness-of-fit tests for categorical frequency data, subject to expected-count conditions.
  2. Tests of independence or association in contingency tables, with appropriate sampling and expected-frequency assumptions.
  3. Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests for the variance of a normally distributed population.

Probability density function

X = Σᵢ₌₁ᵛ Zᵢ², Zᵢ independently ~ N(0,1); f(x) = x^{ν/2−1} e^{−x/2} / [2^{ν/2} Γ(ν/2)], x>0

Degrees of freedom: ν is the number of independent squared standard normal components. For a normal sample, (n−1)S²/σ² follows χ²(n−1).

Beta Distribution of First Kind

History

The beta function has roots in the work of Leonhard Euler in the eighteenth century. The beta distribution emerged as a flexible continuous probability model on a bounded interval and became important in Bayesian statistics, reliability analysis, and modelling proportions. Its shape can range from U-shaped to uniform-like or bell-shaped depending on its two positive shape parameters.

Three real-life applications

  1. Proportions and rates: modelling an unknown success probability, such as the conversion rate of a website.
  2. Quality control: modelling the fraction of defective items or the proportion of a batch meeting a specification.
  3. Public health and ecology: representing a proportion such as vaccination coverage or habitat occupancy.

Distribution, parameters and support

For X following a Beta distribution of the first kind with parameters n > 0 and m > 0:

f(x) = xn−1(1−x)m−1 / B(n,m),   0 < x < 1

Parameters: n and m are positive shape parameters. Support: 0 < x < 1. The beta function is B(n,m) = Γ(n)Γ(m) / Γ(n+m).

Mean: n/(n+m).   Variance: nm / [(n+m)²(n+m+1)].

Statistical Properties — Beta Distribution of First Kind

Parameters: n>0, m>0; support 0<x<1.

Mean: n/(n+m)

Shape: n=m gives symmetry; n>m typically shifts mass toward 1, while n<m typically shifts mass toward 0.

Beta Distribution of Second Kind

History

The beta distribution of the second kind, also called the beta-prime distribution, is obtained by transforming a beta-distributed variable from the interval (0,1) to the positive half-line. It is connected to classical beta and gamma functions and is used for positive, right-skewed quantities where a long upper tail is plausible.

Three real-life applications

  1. Insurance and finance: modelling positive loss amounts or risk ratios with a long right tail.
  2. Reliability and engineering: modelling positive lifetime-related ratios or variation in component performance.
  3. Environmental studies: modelling positive concentration or duration ratios that show strong right skew.

Distribution, parameters and support

For X following a Beta distribution of the second kind with parameters n > 0 and m > 0:

f(x) = xn−1(1+x)−(n+m) / B(n,m),   x > 0

Parameters: n and m are positive shape parameters. Support: x > 0. The density is defined on the positive half-line and is generally right-skewed.

Mean: n/(m−1), for m > 1.   Variance: n(n+m−1) / [(m−2)(m−1)²], for m > 2.

Standard deviation: square root of the variance, only for m>2.

Median: generally no elementary closed form; often obtained numerically.

Mode: (n−1)/(m+1) when n>1; for n≤1 the density has its mode at the boundary x=0 (as a limiting boundary mode).

Skewness: 2(2n+m−1)√(m−2)/[(m−3)√(n(n+m−1))], only for m>3.

Kurtosis: finite only for m>4; excess kurtosis is finite only for m>4. Exact expressions are lengthy, so use the beta-prime central-moment formula or statistical software.

Shape property: positive support with a typically long right tail; moments cease to exist when m is too small.

Gamma Distribution

Continuous probability model · Positive support · Waiting times and accumulated event durations

Statistical Distribution and Parameters

History

The Gamma function has roots in Leonhard Euler’s eighteenth-century work extending the factorial function beyond positive integers. The Gamma distribution later became an important model for positive continuous quantities, including sums of exponential waiting times. It is widely used in probability, reliability, queueing, life testing and Bayesian statistics.

Three real-life applications
  1. Waiting times: total time until a specified number of events occurs in a Poisson process.
  2. Reliability: positive component lifetimes, repair durations and accumulated operating time.
  3. Environmental and biomedical measurements: positive rainfall amounts, pollutant concentrations and selected right-skewed measurements.

Weibull Distribution

A flexible continuous model for lifetime, reliability, and time-to-event data

Statistical Model and Parameters

History

The distribution is named after Swedish engineer and mathematician Waloddi Weibull, who developed and popularized its use in material-strength and lifetime analysis during the twentieth century. His 1951 paper helped establish the Weibull model as a practical tool for reliability engineering. The distribution is now widely used in engineering, survival analysis and environmental modelling.

Three real-life examples
  1. Reliability engineering: time until a bearing, turbine, or electronic component fails.
  2. Medical and survival studies: modelling time to an event, when a Weibull survival model is appropriate.
  3. Wind and materials analysis: wind-speed distributions and variability in material strength.

For location θ ∈ ℝ, scale α > 0 and shape λ > 0, the three-parameter Weibull density is

f(x) = (λ/α) ((x−θ)/α)λ−1 exp(−((x−θ)/α)λ),   x ≥ θ

Support: x ≥ θ. The density is zero for x < θ. The CDF is F(x) = 1 − exp(−((x−θ)/α)λ) for x ≥ θ.

Laplace OR Double Exponential Distribution

A symmetric continuous distribution with a sharp central peak and exponential tails

Statistical Distribution and Parameters

History

The distribution is named after Pierre-Simon Laplace, who studied it in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in connection with probability and errors. Its modern “double exponential” form reflects the fact that the density decreases exponentially on both sides of its location parameter. It is used as a model for symmetric data with heavier tails than the normal distribution.

Three real-life applications
  1. Robust statistics: modelling symmetric residuals with heavier tails than normal errors.
  2. Signal processing and communications: modelling some impulsive or sharply peaked noise distributions.
  3. Finance and risk analysis: modelling selected symmetric return or forecast-error distributions with relatively heavy tails.

For location μ ∈ ℝ and scale λ > 0, the Laplace density is

f(x) = (1 / (2λ)) exp(−|x−μ| / λ),   −∞ < x < ∞

Location: μ is the center. Scale: λ controls spread. The distribution is symmetric about μ.
